How an insolvency case actually moves through the NCLT — statute, reality, reform.

The IBC promised a 330-day resolution timeline. The data shows what really happens: where the time goes, where the system bleeds, and what two distinct strands of reform — the 2026 Amendment Act and the broader reform agenda — would change.

Coverage: Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) under IBC, 2016 Last updated: 22 May 2026 Primary sources: IBBI · NCLT · Supreme Court · DAKSH · BLRC
§01 — The Process

The statutory journey of a CIRP, step by step.

From the moment a default occurs to the moment a resolution plan is implemented (or liquidation ordered), an insolvency case under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 passes through fourteen distinct procedural steps. Each step is governed by a section of the Code and a statutory time limit. This is how it is meant to work on paper.

§02 — The Reality

Three versions of the same process.

The same five-stage CIRP journey, seen three different ways: as the Code originally promised, as it actually unfolds today, and as the 2026 Amendment Act now demands. A fourth bar shows the new CIIRP alternative the Amendment introduces. Each tells a different part of the story; together they show where India's insolvency regime stands and how far the 2026 reforms intend to bring it back.

How to read this. The bars share the same horizontal scale (0–2000 days). The statutory bar (gold) and the amendment bar (teal) both end well under 250 days — both target the original 330-day cap. The actual bar (coral) tells the policy story: real-world cases run roughly six times the statutory promise. The 2026 Amendment Act now demands what the original IBC implied, plus a mandatory reason-giving regime for delay; whether those timelines are achievable in practice depends on tribunal capacity — what we look at in Costs & Consequences.

What's not shown. Liquidation cases (which 32% of admitted cases reach) take a further 508 days on average from admission to liquidation order, plus 646 days to final report (IBBI, Mar 2025). Variance between reporting periods is real: IBBI's Jan–Mar 2025 newsletter reports 597-day CIRP average; the Jul–Sep 2025 newsletter shows it has risen to 725 days. DAKSH's State of Tribunals 2025 cites 752 days. We show the most recent IBBI figure as the headline and note variance throughout.
§03 — Outcomes

What happens to admitted cases.

A resolution plan is the headline outcome the IBC was designed to deliver. In practice, only about one in seven admitted cases reaches a resolution plan. More cases end in liquidation than in resolution. The framework is doing useful work even where it doesn't culminate in resolution — settlements and withdrawals account for over a quarter of closures — but the gap between intention and outcome is the second strand of the policy story.

The recovery picture
When resolution does happen, the numbers are strong.

Of the 1,300 cases resolved by plan, creditors realised 170% of liquidation value and 32.8% of admitted claims on average.

In 104 of those cases, financial creditors recovered 100% of admitted claims. The mechanism works — when it gets to work.

Source: IBBI Quarterly Newsletter, Jan–Mar 2025

§04 — The Liquidation Track

When resolution fails, liquidation is its own long journey.

For the 32% of admitted cases that end in liquidation, the timeline doesn't stop at the liquidation order — it extends through asset sale, claim distribution, and the eventual submission of a final report. Together, these stages add more than three years on top of the failed CIRP. The data here is unforgiving.

§05 — Methodology

What the numbers measure, and what they don't.

Every figure on this page comes from a primary source. Where sources disagree, we show the variance. Where data is unavailable, we say so. Below are the most important methodological notes for anyone using this page to cite, report on, or build further analysis.

The "597 days" caveat

The IBBI's headline CIRP average excludes time excluded by the Adjudicating Authority — pauses on the statutory clock for litigation, exclusions under Sec. 12(3), and other authorised stoppages. Real elapsed wall-clock time is longer.

Why sources disagree

IBBI Mar 2025 reports 597-day CIRP average. IBBI Sep 2025 reports 725. DAKSH 2025 cites 752. All correct, all measuring slightly different cohorts at slightly different times. We use the most recent IBBI figure as the lead and surface variance.

Pre-admission data

The 468–650 day pre-admission range is from IBBI data cited in the MCA Standing Committee on Finance report and confirmed in independent practitioner analyses. IBBI does not publish a single official pre-admission average; the range reflects Sec 9 data for FY20–21 and FY21–22.

Plan approval delays

The 48–738 day range for plan approval pendency comes from the Supreme Court's 29 April 2026 suo motu order. As of that date, 383 plans were pending NCLT approval after CoC clearance.

What's not measured

IBBI does not publish stage-wise timing for the intermediate CIRP steps (claims invitation, CoC meetings, EoI invitations, plan evaluation). Where we name those stages in §01, the times are statutory targets, not measured averages. No public dataset tracks these granularly.

Refresh cadence

This page is updated each quarter as IBBI publishes new newsletters (typically May, August, November, February). The "Data as of" badge in the top-right reflects the most recent IBBI quarterly cutoff applied.
