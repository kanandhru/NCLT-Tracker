§ Costs & Consequences

What the delays actually cost.

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code rewrote India's credit discipline — bad loans fell from 11.2% of bank assets in 2018 to 2.58% in 2025. But the system that delivered that turnaround now runs on tribunal capacity that the Supreme Court has called “grim and dismal”. ₹24.72 lakh crore of commercial disputes — roughly 7.5% of India's GDP — sits unresolved before just 350 members. This is what the delay costs, who is paying, and what the experts have been saying about it.

The single most important point on this page
The IBC is the central reason bank gross NPAs collapsed from 11.2% in 2018 to 2.58% in 2025. The system worked. The system continues to work where it gets to operate. The crisis is no longer in the law — it is in the institutional capacity to deliver what the law promises.
§02 — Value erosion

Delay is the cost.

Insolvency is a race against value decay. As cases drag, assets depreciate, employees leave, customers shift to competitors, and supplier relationships break down. The empirical relationship between resolution speed and creditor recovery is one of the cleanest in the data.

The 6-month figure (~45%) approximately equals the global median creditor recovery; the 2-year+ figure (~15%) approaches liquidation territory. The actual NCLT average CIRP timeline of 853 days places the typical case in the lowest recovery band.

The Standing Committee on Finance, in its 32nd Report (2021), identified this directly: "resolution period delays result in rapid value erosion, thereby reducing the realization value." At the time, the Committee found 71% of pending IBC cases had been pending more than 180 days, with approximately ₹9 lakh crore of admitted claims tied up.

Five years later, the numbers have grown. The Economic Survey 2025–26 places the average CIRP at 853 days for FY25 cases — 158% over the statutory 330-day cap. The cost is paid by financial creditors taking 67% haircuts on admitted claims, by operational creditors waiting years for payment, and by the wider credit ecosystem absorbing the signal that resolution is slow.

The same IIM Ahmedabad study that documents the spectacular post-resolution turnaround — resolved firms see 89% sales growth, 106% capex jump, and market capitalisation tripling over five years — identifies a non-linear relationship between resolution time and expenses. In the Wholesale & Retail Trade sector, complexity drove expense ratios to 47% of realisable amount, the highest of any industry studied.

The upside, in other words, is real but conditional. Every day a case sits in pendency compounds the gap between what the system can deliver and what it does deliver.

§04 — The diagnoses

What the experts have been saying.

Three independent bodies of analysis — the legislature, the academic-policy community, and the judiciary — have arrived at substantially the same diagnosis. The remarkable thing is how consistent the critique has been across the last four years.

Parliament
3 Aug 2021

"Main reasons for delay in the insolvency resolution process are delays in admission of cases in NCLT and delays in approval of Resolution Plans by the NCLT. About 13,170 IBC cases are pending… 71% have been pending for more than 180 days. Resolution period delays result in rapid value erosion."

Standing Committee on Finance, 32nd Report — Implementation of IBC: Pitfalls and Solutions. View report →

DAKSH
25 Sep 2025

"Tribunals were meant to deliver speed, expertise, and accessibility. In practice, they have created a fragmented system, raised persistent concerns about independence, and delivered outcomes no better than courts."

DAKSH, State of Tribunals Report 2025. ₹24.72 lakh crore locked up; 88% NCLT staff on contract; 84.9% at NCLAT; 752-day average disposal. Full report →

Supreme Court
29 Apr 2026

"Grim and dismal." 383 resolution plans pending nationwide. Contractual staffing at this level called "unheard of" for a quasi-judicial body.

Supreme Court of India, suo motu cognisance — Bench of Justices Pardiwala and Viswanathan. Reporting: LiveLaw, Bar & Bench, Mint.

Economic Survey
Jan 2026

"The institutional constraint is present at the level of the courts as well as at the level of the Resolution Professionals. Only 30 NCLT benches handle cases across IBC and Companies Act jurisdictions, and RPs are also in short number. Of 4,527 registered RPs, only 2,198 (49%) hold active Authorisation for Assignment."

Government of India, Economic Survey 2025–26. Coverage →

§06 — The wider stakes

What the IBC has delivered — and what's at risk.

It would be misleading to read this page as an indictment of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code. The Code has been one of the most consequential reforms of the last decade. The question is whether its gains can be sustained when the adjudicating institution is operating well below its design capacity.

Bank gross NPAs since the IBC
Scheduled commercial banks · % of total advances · RBI / PIB
Source: Press Information Bureau, July 2025 · RBI data. The IBC is identified by both the RBI and Government of India as the central driver of this trajectory.

NPAs at 11.2% in 2018 were a balance-sheet emergency. NPAs at 2.58% in 2025 are a healthy banking system. The IBC's "creditor in control" framework, the §29A bar on defaulting promoters, the credible threat of CIRP — together these changed borrower behaviour as much as they recovered specific loans.

The Reserve Bank of India's Deputy Governor has noted that 17 of the 41 large RBI-directed CIRPs have been resolved, realising 50% of admitted claims and 190% of liquidation value — a rate of return that vindicates the model.

What the data on this page argues is narrower: the delivery institution is the binding constraint. The 2026 Amendment Act mandates faster timelines — 30-day plan-approval caps, mandatory recording of reasons for delay, the creditor-initiated CIIRP path. Whether those timelines are achievable depends on tribunal capacity that DAKSH, Parliament, and now the Supreme Court have called grossly inadequate.

The reforms address the law. The reforms do not, in themselves, address the people, premises, and posts that the law depends on.
